PMP Scenario Question Patterns: Obstacles, Business Case, Charter, and Change Requests

Four common PMP scenario patterns are less about memorizing a term and more about recognizing what kind of decision the project manager is allowed to make next.

1. When the project hits a major obstacle, look for an alternative path

Some PMP questions describe a project that runs into a serious obstacle: a technical limitation, an unexpected risk, a resource shortage, a supplier problem, or a delivery approach that no longer works as planned. When the wording makes the obstacle sound significant, the best answer often involves identifying alternatives, developing options, or recommending a feasible solution path.

The important detail is that the exam usually does not ask you to invent a specific technical fix. It asks what a responsible project manager should do next. In that situation, avoid answers that jump straight to implementation, blame a team member, or escalate without analysis. A better answer will usually say that the project manager should work with the team, assess options, identify an alternative, or present possible solutions to the right decision maker.

This pattern is different from a routine change request. A normal change question usually points toward documenting the request and following integrated change control. A major-obstacle question often signals that the project needs a practical way forward before a decision can be made. The wording may include phrases such as critical barrier, severe constraint, no longer feasible, resource unavailable, or technical approach will not work. Those phrases should make you slow down and look for an option that creates a path, not an answer that pretends the original plan is still fine.

2. When the question asks whether to start or continue, think business case

If a scenario asks whether a project should begin, continue, pause, or remain justified, the business case is usually the anchor. The business case explains why the project is worth doing. It connects the project to expected benefits, organizational need, strategic value, cost justification, and business viability.

Many candidates miss this pattern because they look for a schedule or scope answer. Those may matter later, but the start-or-continue decision is not mainly a scheduling question. It is a value question. If the expected benefit has disappeared, if the market need has changed, or if the organization is unsure whether the project still makes sense, the project manager should not simply push the team harder. The responsible move is to review the business justification, consult the appropriate stakeholders, and support a decision based on value.

On the exam, be careful with answers that say to continue because work has already started. Sunk effort does not justify future investment. Also be careful with answers that say the project manager should personally cancel the project. The project manager normally provides information, analysis, and recommendations. The sponsor or governance body usually owns the decision to authorize, continue, or terminate the project.

3. When the scenario points to a new project, high-level authority, or high-level risk, check the project charter

The project charter appears in PMP questions more often than candidates expect. Watch for clues such as a new project, a newly assigned project manager, high-level requirements, high-level risks, initial constraints, sponsor authority, or the project manager's authority and responsibility. These are charter-level signals.

The project charter does not contain a detailed project management plan. It is a high-level authorization document. It may summarize the project purpose, measurable objectives, high-level scope, high-level requirements, assumptions, constraints, high-level risks, milestone summary, budget summary, key stakeholders, sponsor information, and the project manager's authority. The key phrase is high-level. If an answer describes detailed schedule activities, detailed cost estimates, or a full communication strategy, it may belong in the project management plan rather than the charter.

The charter also matters because it links the project to the sponsor and the performing organization. It gives the project manager the authority to apply organizational resources to project work. In some questions, the charter may also define approval expectations, including who accepts the project or authorizes closure. If the scenario is about early authorization, sponsor alignment, or the project manager's authority, the charter is usually more relevant than a detailed subsidiary plan.

4. Before a change request is approved, do not skip the change process

Change questions are a major source of lost PMP points because several answer choices can sound reasonable. Before a change is approved, the project manager should protect the process. That usually means documenting the request, performing impact analysis, preparing options if needed, and sending the request through the defined change control path.

There is one useful distinction. If a powerful stakeholder suggests a change that would create major disruption or poor value, the project manager may first discuss the impact and help the stakeholder understand alternatives. For example, the better solution may be to start a separate initiative rather than destabilize the current project. This is not the same as secretly rejecting the change. It is professional influence before formal action, based on facts and value.

Once a change is formally requested, the sequence matters. If the answer choices include both documenting the change request and analyzing the impact, the safer PMP answer is often to document or submit the change request first, then analyze impact through the proper process. Impact analysis is necessary, but it should not replace formal change documentation. After the impact is understood, the change may go to the change control board or another authorized decision maker, depending on the project governance structure.

Avoid answers that implement the change immediately, approve it because the customer is important, ignore it because it is inconvenient, or escalate before basic analysis has been done. The exam favors controlled transparency: write it down, assess it, identify options, and let the correct authority approve or reject it.

How to practice these four patterns

When reviewing PMP practice questions, do not only mark whether you chose A, B, C, or D. Label the pattern. Was it a major obstacle, a business case question, a charter clue, or a pre-approval change request? This turns isolated questions into reusable exam judgment.

A simple review table works well. Use four columns: scenario clue, decision type, best next step, and trap answer. The trap answer is important. It might be acting too quickly, escalating too early, approving without authority, or choosing a detailed plan when the question is asking for high-level authorization. After 20 to 30 questions, you will start seeing the same logic repeat with different project stories.

The PMP exam does not reward dramatic action. It rewards disciplined action. Understand the situation, respect authority, protect business value, and follow the process before execution.
