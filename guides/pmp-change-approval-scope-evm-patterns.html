PMP Change Request and Earned Value Patterns

Change control and earned value questions look mechanical at first, but the PMP exam still tests sequence, authority, and disciplined reasoning.

1. After a change request is approved, follow the order

Once a change request has been approved, the project manager should not treat the approval as the end of the process. Approval creates a new obligation: the project records and plans must reflect the decision, the right people must know what changed, and the approved change must be implemented and verified.

A reliable PMP sequence is: update the change log, update the relevant project management plan or project documents, notify affected stakeholders, implement the approved change, and confirm that the approved change was implemented correctly. The first three steps are especially common in exam questions because they test whether you understand that approval must be controlled and communicated before the work simply moves forward.

Be careful with answer choices that jump straight to implementation. If the question says the change has just been approved, the next best step may be to update records or communicate the decision, not to start the work immediately. The exact answer depends on what has already happened in the scenario. If the change log and baselines have already been updated and the team has been informed, implementation may be the next step. If the question does not say those steps happened, the safer answer usually protects the control process first.

2. Change questions often include answers you should almost never choose

Change-control questions regularly include tempting choices that sound efficient but violate the process. The first trap is rejecting the change immediately. The answer may give a reasonable-sounding reason, such as schedule pressure or limited budget, but the project manager normally should not dismiss a formal change without documentation and analysis.

The second trap is ignoring the change and continuing with the current plan. That may sound disciplined, but it fails to engage the stakeholder request and fails to protect transparency. The third trap is delaying the change without a reason, such as saying the team will discuss it in the next meeting or handle it in a later phase. Delay is not a process.

The fourth trap is implementing the change directly. Unless the scenario clearly says the change has already passed through the defined integrated change control process and the project is now at the implementation step, direct implementation is usually wrong. The fifth trap is directly updating the project management plan just because a request was made. Plans and baselines are updated after the change has been evaluated and approved through the proper authority.

When two answers seem close, ask which one preserves governance. PMP answers usually favor documentation, impact analysis, approval by the correct authority, and communication before execution.

3. Requirement and scope problems are usually scope change questions

When a scenario says that a requirement was missed, an error was discovered, the work does not meet customer expectations, the deliverable does not match the plan, or the customer introduces a new requirement, you are usually in scope-change territory. The exam may describe the problem in different language, but the underlying issue is that the agreed scope or requirements may need to change.

For this type of question, look for answers that follow change control: document the request, analyze the impact, review the relevant plans, submit the request for approval, or follow the change management process. Avoid answers that simply add the requirement, reject the customer, or instruct the team to absorb the work without analysis.

A useful document distinction also appears on the exam. If a scope-change question asks which plan to reference, and both the scope management plan and the change management plan appear as answer choices, the change management plan is often the stronger answer. The scope management plan explains how scope is defined, validated, and controlled. It can include how scope changes are handled. But the change management plan defines how changes are formally monitored and controlled across the project. If the question is primarily about how to process a change, the change management plan is usually the better fit.

4. Earned value questions become easier when you write the known variables first

Earned value management questions look intimidating because the options may contain several formulas. The safest habit is simple: write down the known variables first. Identify PV, EV, AC, BAC, CPI, SPI, or any other values given in the question. Once the variables are clear, most questions need only one or two formulas.

For performance questions, remember the basic ratios. CPI equals EV divided by AC. SPI equals EV divided by PV. If CPI is below 1.0, cost performance is unfavorable. If SPI is below 1.0, schedule performance is unfavorable. The PMP exam may not always ask for the label; it may ask what the project manager should conclude or what corrective action should be considered.

TCPI is easier to remember if you connect it to CPI. CPI is EV divided by AC. TCPI adds the idea of future required performance. A common formula is TCPI = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC). A quick memory hook is that TCPI adds the unfinished work and remaining budget view: what work remains, divided by what budget remains. Depending on the scenario, TCPI may also be calculated against a revised estimate, but the exam wording should tell you which target is being used.

Some schedule questions ask for a rough completion time. If BAC is represented as total planned duration or can be converted into days, a simplified approach may use SPI to estimate the adjusted duration. For example, planned duration divided by SPI gives a rough performance-adjusted duration. Use this carefully: it is a shortcut for exam reasoning, not a replacement for formal scheduling analysis.

How to practice these four patterns

For change-control questions, do not memorize one answer. Memorize the order. Before approval, document and analyze. After approval, update records and plans, inform stakeholders, implement, and verify. For scope and requirement problems, train yourself to ask whether the scenario is really describing a scope change. For earned value, write variables before touching a formula.

A good review note is short: trigger, process, next step, trap. For example: customer adds requirement, scope change, submit and analyze through change control, trap is implementing immediately. Or: CPI and SPI given, interpret performance before recommending action, trap is using the wrong denominator. These compact notes make the pattern easier to recognize under exam pressure.

The goal is not to become mechanical. The goal is to become orderly. PMP scenario questions reward candidates who can protect process without becoming slow, communicate decisions without overstepping authority, and use formulas only after the question data is clear.
