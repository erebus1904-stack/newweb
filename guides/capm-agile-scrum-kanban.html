CAPM agile frameworks

CAPM Agile Questions: Scrum, Kanban, Iterations, and Retrospectives

Agile frameworks and methodologies make up 20% of CAPM. The exam does not expect you to run a perfect Scrum team on paper, but it does expect you to recognize roles, events, flow, feedback, and backlog priority in plain scenarios.

Why agile needs its own study block

The agile domain is 20% of CAPM, which is large enough to deserve focused review. New candidates often treat agile as a vocabulary chapter: memorize Scrum, Kanban, sprint, backlog, and retrospective, then move on. That approach leaves points on the table because exam questions usually test judgment. The wording may describe a blocked developer, a stakeholder asking for a new feature, or a backlog full of unclear items. You need to know who acts, what event fits, and what principle is being protected.

A useful way to study is to stop asking, "What is the definition?" and start asking, "What would a healthy agile team do next?" Agile questions usually reward transparency, frequent inspection, adaptation, collaboration with the customer, and work pulled from a prioritized backlog. They usually punish command-and-control answers, hidden work, skipping feedback, changing sprint scope casually, or letting the loudest stakeholder override value-based priority.

Product Owner: value and backlog priority

The Product Owner is one of the highest-value roles to understand for CAPM. In exam language, this person owns product value and backlog priority. That does not mean the Product Owner writes every requirement alone or tells every team member how to work. It means the Product Owner keeps the product backlog ordered so the team is working on the most valuable, useful, or risk-reducing items at the right time.

If a scenario says several stakeholders want different features, look for an answer that has the Product Owner clarify value, dependencies, risk, and customer need before reordering the backlog. If a team member asks which item should be worked on next, the answer is not usually "ask the project manager to assign tasks." In Scrum, the team pulls work from a prioritized backlog and plans the sprint around a goal.

A common trap is confusing stakeholder pressure with priority. A senior stakeholder may request a feature, but the best answer still checks product value and backlog order. CAPM questions often use this to see whether you understand that agile is responsive but not chaotic.

Scrum Master: servant leadership and impediment removal

The Scrum Master supports the team by coaching Scrum, helping remove impediments, and protecting useful collaboration. On the exam, the Scrum Master is not a task boss. This role does not assign every ticket, judge individual performance in the Daily Scrum, or bypass the Product Owner to reorder the backlog. The Scrum Master helps the team use the framework well and improves the environment around the team.

If a developer is blocked by a dependency, the Scrum Master may help make the impediment visible and work with the right people to remove it. If stakeholders interrupt the team during a sprint with constant side requests, the Scrum Master may coach the team and stakeholders on the agreed process. If the team skips retrospectives because everyone is busy, the Scrum Master should help restore the inspect-and-adapt habit instead of treating it as optional ceremony.

For CAPM, remember the phrase servant leader. In answer choices, that often looks like facilitating, coaching, removing obstacles, encouraging self-organization, or helping the team improve. It rarely looks like commanding, blaming, micromanaging, or escalating before the team understands the problem.

Daily Scrum: coordination, not a manager status meeting

The Daily Scrum is short, regular coordination for the team. It helps the team inspect progress toward the sprint goal and plan the next day of work. CAPM candidates lose easy points when they read it as a traditional status meeting for the manager. The better mental model is team synchronization.

If an answer choice says the project manager should use the Daily Scrum to collect detailed individual status reports, be careful. If the team is using Scrum, the event is for the team to coordinate. The Scrum Master may ensure the event happens and stays useful, but the team owns the conversation. Detailed problem solving can happen after the Daily Scrum with the people who need to stay.

In scenario questions, Daily Scrum is a strong answer when the issue is day-to-day alignment or surfacing blockers. It is a weak answer when the issue is product acceptance, stakeholder feedback, or process improvement after an iteration. Those belong elsewhere.

Sprint Review versus Retrospective

CAPM questions often test the difference between Sprint Review and Retrospective. The Sprint Review inspects the product increment with stakeholders. The team shows completed work, receives feedback, discusses what changed, and uses that learning to inform future backlog decisions. If the scenario involves customer feedback, feature acceptance, product direction, or whether the increment meets expectations, think Sprint Review.

The Retrospective inspects the team's way of working. The product is not the main subject; the process is. The team discusses what went well, what did not go well, and what experiment or improvement should be tried next. If the scenario describes recurring defects, poor collaboration, missed handoffs, unclear working agreements, or a team that wants to improve its own process, think Retrospective.

One practical rule helps: review the product with stakeholders; retrospect on the team's process with the team. When the exam gives two similar answers, this rule usually separates them. A stakeholder complaint about a feature does not belong in a private process-only discussion first. A team problem with communication should not be solved by turning the Sprint Review into a blame session.

Kanban: visualize flow and limit work in progress

Kanban questions have a different rhythm from Scrum questions. Kanban is usually about visualizing work, managing flow, limiting work in progress, and improving continuously. A Kanban board makes work visible as it moves across states such as To Do, In Progress, Review, and Done. Work-in-progress limits help prevent the team from starting too many items and finishing too few.

If a scenario says the team has many tasks started but little completed, look for an answer about limiting WIP or improving flow. If the team cannot see where work is stuck, visualizing the workflow is likely relevant. If work arrives continuously and does not fit neatly into fixed-length sprints, Kanban may be a better clue than Scrum.

Do not assume every agile question is Scrum. CAPM may describe a team using pull-based work, a board, cycle time, or WIP limits. Those words point toward Kanban. The mindset is still agile: make work visible, reduce bottlenecks, inspect flow, and improve based on evidence.

Iterations, increments, and backlog refinement

An iteration is a short, timeboxed period where a team produces a usable increment or meaningful progress toward one. In Scrum, this is the sprint. CAPM questions may use either word. Focus less on the label and more on the cycle: plan a small batch, do the work, inspect the result, learn, and adapt.

Backlog refinement supports that cycle by making future work clearer. The team and Product Owner may split large items, clarify acceptance criteria, estimate, and identify dependencies. Refinement does not mean locking the entire future plan. It means preparing enough upcoming work so the team can make good sprint or flow decisions.

If a question says the team repeatedly starts items that are too vague, refinement is likely part of the answer. If a question says stakeholders learned something from a recent increment, the backlog may need to be reordered. Agile planning is real planning, but it accepts that learning changes the plan.

How to practice agile CAPM questions

When you review agile practice questions, tag each missed answer with one of four labels: role, event, artifact, or principle. Role mistakes usually confuse Product Owner, Scrum Master, and team responsibilities. Event mistakes usually mix up Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, and Retrospective. Artifact mistakes usually involve product backlog, sprint backlog, increment, board, or Definition of Done. Principle mistakes involve value, transparency, adaptation, self-organization, or flow.

Use short rules, not long notes. Write "PO orders backlog by value," "Scrum Master removes impediments," "Daily Scrum coordinates the team," "Review checks product feedback," "Retro improves team process," and "Kanban limits WIP." Then answer another small set of questions and see whether you apply the rule faster.

The CAPM agile domain is manageable if you study it as behavior, not decoration. Know the terms, but keep asking what a team would do next to improve transparency, protect value, reduce blockers, and learn from real feedback. That is the level where many exam questions are written.
